I Built My Own Language Model. Here's What Happened.

By Jim Morissens · October 25, 2026

I've been fascinated by artificial intelligence for quite some time. Not just what it can do, but how it actually works.

We have AI models that can write software, solve complex problems and have conversations that sometimes feel surprisingly human.

But I kept coming back to one question.

What if I built one myself? From absolutely nothing?

Not downloading an existing model. Not fine-tuning Llama. Actually building the architecture, training it and figuring out what works and what doesn't.

So that's what I did.

And naturally, I underestimated how much I'd learn along the way.

Meet OLAMB

I called my model OLAMB — Open Learning Architecture & Model Builder.

The name is a little joke. Meta has Llama, I have OLAMB. And since my first experiments involved sheep and lambs, it seemed like the obvious choice.

But there's a more serious idea behind it.

I don't want to build just one language model. I want to understand how different architectures learn, why some work better than others and whether I can eventually design better ones myself.

I'm not trying to compete with OpenAI or Meta. That would be a rather ambitious project for a guy experimenting on his own computer.

I'm interested in what's happening under the hood.

Teaching a Computer to Talk About Sheep

My first model had roughly one million parameters.

For comparison, modern large language models can have billions. Mine was practically microscopic.

I started with a small collection of English sentences about animals, places and simple actions.

After training, OLAMB started producing things like:

“the lamb near the river.”

Not exactly Shakespeare, but I was genuinely pleased.

Then it generated:

“the fierd.”

Less impressive.

And at one point, I discovered the model couldn't even recognize the letter K.

Why? Because that letter hadn't appeared in the original training vocabulary.

I'd built a language model that couldn't handle the entire alphabet.

Brilliant.

But that's exactly why I wanted to do this myself. When you use an existing AI model, someone else has already solved thousands of these little problems.

When you start from scratch, you get to discover every single one of them.

Sometimes the hard way.

Bigger Isn't Always Better

I kept experimenting.

One version grew to about 2.7 million parameters. It generated recognizable pieces of English, but the results were still inconsistent.

So I decided to go in the opposite direction.

Instead of adding more parameters, I built a smaller model with a better-controlled training dataset.

The latest version, OLAMB V3, has just 817,181 parameters.

It uses a four-layer Transformer architecture with four attention heads. Small enough to train on a regular CPU, but complex enough to experiment with the fundamentals of modern language models.

This morning, October 25, I completed a fresh training run of 500 iterations.

The validation loss dropped from 3.50 to 0.64.

That's a substantial improvement in the model's ability to predict the next token in the validation data.

Does it mean OLAMB has suddenly become intelligent?

Of course not.

It might simply have become very good at recognizing the patterns in its training material.

And that's what I want to find out next.

I've deliberately kept part of the dataset separate, including combinations of words the model hasn't encountered during training.

Now I want to see whether OLAMB can work with those unfamiliar combinations.

Because there's a big difference between remembering something and learning how something works.

But OLAMB Isn't the Whole Story

Here's something I haven't mentioned yet.

OLAMB isn't actually where this journey started.

I've already built another experimental AI platform called ORIGIN.

And while OLAMB is about building and training models, ORIGIN has a rather different purpose.

In fact, one of the reasons I'm developing OLAMB is to put ORIGIN itself to the test.

I wanted a model I could control from the ground up. Something I could change, deliberately challenge, break and rebuild. Something that produces real experimental evidence rather than impressive-looking demonstrations.

OLAMB gives me exactly that.

The interesting question isn't just whether OLAMB can learn.

It's whether ORIGIN can help me discover why it learns, recognize what isn't working and guide the next experiment.

And perhaps, eventually, suggest something I wouldn't have thought of myself.

ORIGIN already exists, and I've been running experiments with it. Some of the results have been interesting enough to make me rethink where I want to take this.

But I'll save that story for another post.

Because ORIGIN raises a much bigger question than whether a small language model can learn to finish a sentence.

What happens when you start experimenting with the research process itself?

That's where things get particularly interesting.

So, What Have I Actually Achieved?

I've built and trained my own small language models from scratch.

I've watched them go from random predictions to producing recognizable language patterns.

I've broken things, fixed things and occasionally discovered that something as simple as a missing letter can ruin your afternoon.

And I'm using those models to challenge an experimental research platform I've already built.

I'm not claiming I've invented a new form of intelligence. I haven't.

I'm also not a university researcher with a laboratory full of GPUs.

I'm a technology guy who's spent years working with infrastructure and complex systems, and who became curious enough about AI to start building it himself.

The more I learn, the more questions I seem to have.

And strangely enough, that's what keeps me going.

What's Next?

First, I want to find out what OLAMB V3 has really learned.

Then I'll experiment with different architectures, better training methods and see how far I can push the interaction between OLAMB and ORIGIN.

I'm particularly interested in whether we can go beyond simply making existing models bigger and start exploring different ways for them to learn.

Will any of this lead to something genuinely new?

I have no idea.

But I think it's worth finding out.

And that's really the point of this journal.

Not to pretend I have all the answers, but to share what happens when I start asking questions and actually try to build something.

It started with a sheep.

Now I'm curious where the lamb will take me.

And in an upcoming post, I'll introduce ORIGIN properly — including why I built it, what it's already doing and the rather ambitious question I'm trying to answer with it.
