What If AI Could Discover Something We Haven't Thought Of Yet?

By Jim Morissens · October 26, 2026

In my previous post, I introduced OLAMB, my homemade language model that started its life learning about sheep and occasionally inventing words that don't exist.

But there's something I haven't really explained yet.

OLAMB wasn't actually where this journey started.

Before I decided to build my own language model, I was already working on something considerably more ambitious.

ORIGIN.

And while OLAMB is about understanding how machines learn, ORIGIN started with a question that's been occupying my mind for quite some time.

What if the way we're building artificial intelligence today is only one of many possibilities?

I don't mean making existing models bigger, faster or cheaper.

I mean something more fundamental.

What if there are entirely different ways for machines to learn, reason or process information that we simply haven't discovered yet?

I don't know the answer.

But apparently, I'm the kind of person who can't leave a question like that alone.

So, What Is ORIGIN?

ORIGIN is an independent experimental AI research platform I've been building.

And it's not just an idea or something I'm planning to develop someday.

ORIGIN already exists. It's running, evolving and being put through experiments of its own.

I started building it because I wanted to explore AI beyond the applications we interact with every day.

Most of us experience artificial intelligence through finished products. We ask questions, generate images, write software or automate tasks.

And that's impressive.

But I'm more interested in what's happening underneath.

Why do certain approaches work so well?

What are their limitations?

And are we overlooking possibilities because we've become comfortable with the methods we already know?

ORIGIN is my attempt to explore some of those questions.

I'm deliberately keeping parts of the project private for now. There's still a lot I want to investigate before sharing more about how it works.

But I can explain what motivates it.

Are We Asking the Right Questions?

The progress in artificial intelligence over the past few years has been extraordinary.

Models have become larger, more capable and increasingly useful.

Yet I sometimes wonder whether we're becoming too focused on improving what's already successful.

Don't get me wrong. There's an enormous amount of innovation happening in AI research, including work that challenges established architectures.

But I find myself drawn to a slightly different question.

What if an interesting breakthrough comes from questioning something we've been taking for granted?

History is full of examples where progress didn't come from simply making an existing solution better.

Sometimes someone approached the problem from an entirely different direction.

That doesn't mean every unconventional idea is a good one.

Most probably aren't.

But how do you know until you've investigated?

That's the kind of curiosity that drives ORIGIN.

I'm interested in exploring possibilities, including ones that might initially seem impractical or unlikely.

Not because I expect every unusual idea to succeed.

But because I don't want to assume that everything worth discovering has already been imagined.

Curiosity Is Great. Evidence Is Better.

There's one thing I want to be very clear about.

I'm not interested in building something that produces impressive claims without anything to support them.

AI is already surrounded by enough hype.

An unexpected result can be exciting, but excitement isn't evidence.

If something appears to work, I want to understand why.

If it doesn't, I want to understand that too.

And if an experiment produces something surprising, I want to know whether the result holds up when challenged.

That principle is central to how I approach ORIGIN.

Be ambitious about the questions. Be demanding about the answers.

It sounds simple.

In practice, it's anything but.

Sometimes the most exciting result turns out to have a perfectly ordinary explanation.

Sometimes an apparent failure becomes more interesting than the original hypothesis.

And sometimes you spend hours investigating something only to discover that the problem was embarrassingly obvious.

I've had a few of those moments already.

I suspect there will be plenty more.

Why OLAMB Matters

This is where my other project comes into the picture.

In Journal #001, I explained how I started building OLAMB, my own small language model.

What I didn't fully explain was why.

Yes, I wanted to understand how language models work.

But I also needed something I could experiment with freely.

Something that belonged entirely to my own research environment.

Something I could change, challenge and rebuild without depending on a model someone else had already trained.

OLAMB gives me a way to put ORIGIN to the test.

The two projects are different, but they're closely connected.

OLAMB allows me to investigate learning on a manageable scale, while ORIGIN explores questions that go beyond any single model.

And that's where things start getting interesting.

Because sometimes the question isn't whether a model can produce a better answer.

Sometimes it's whether we've been approaching the problem in the right way at all.

I'll leave that thought there for now.

A Research Lab Without the Lab

I should probably point out that I don't have a university research department behind me.

No team of scientists.

No supercomputer hidden in the basement.

Just my own equipment, years of experience working with technology and a growing collection of questions that keep leading to more questions.

My professional background is in IT infrastructure and engineering, not academic AI research.

So a lot of this involves learning new concepts, studying the mathematics, experimenting and occasionally discovering that something I thought I understood was considerably more complicated.

It's a humbling experience.

But I enjoy it.

I've spent years working with complex systems, solving technical problems and trying to understand why things behave the way they do.

ORIGIN takes that same curiosity into unfamiliar territory.

The difference is that this time, I don't necessarily know what a successful outcome looks like.

And strangely enough, I find that rather motivating.

Has ORIGIN Actually Achieved Anything?

A fair question.

And one I think anyone working on an experimental AI project should be willing to answer.

ORIGIN is already operational, and I've completed several stages of development and experimentation.

Some experiments have produced useful observations. Others have exposed limitations or sent me back to reconsider an earlier assumption.

I've also been working on making the platform more capable of supporting longer-term research rather than treating every experiment as a separate exercise.

But I'm careful about what I call a discovery.

Building a system that investigates difficult questions doesn't mean you've answered them.

And having an interesting experimental result doesn't automatically mean you've invented something new.

ORIGIN hasn't demonstrated a fundamentally new form of intelligence.

I'm not claiming otherwise.

What I have is a working experimental foundation, a research direction I find genuinely fascinating and a growing collection of things I want to investigate.

For now, I'm keeping the more interesting technical details and some ongoing investigations to myself.

If something eventually produces a result worth sharing, I'd rather show the evidence than make an announcement based on an exciting possibility.

What Comes Next?

For the moment, OLAMB and ORIGIN will continue developing alongside each other.

I'll be testing new ideas, questioning assumptions and trying to understand what the results actually tell me.

Some of that work will appear in future journal entries.

Other parts will remain private until there's a good reason to share them.

And I certainly don't expect every experiment to succeed.

In fact, I'd be slightly suspicious if they did.

But there's a bigger question behind all of this that I keep returning to.

What if the next interesting step in AI isn't simply building a more powerful model, but discovering a possibility we haven't seriously considered yet?

Maybe there's nothing there.

Maybe the approaches we already know will prove to be the best ones for the problems I'm investigating.

Or maybe, somewhere along the way, an experiment will produce something I didn't expect.

Something that makes me stop, look at the results and think:

Hang on. Why did that happen?

That's what I'm looking for.

Not a flashy demonstration or another grand claim about artificial intelligence.

Just something genuinely worth investigating.

I'm not building ORIGIN because I know where this leads.

I'm building it because I want to find out.

And for now, that's enough.
